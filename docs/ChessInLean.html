Honesty first

What is known about perfect play

Theorem (Zermelo, 1913)

A finite two-player game with perfect information has a value: one side can force a win, or both can force a draw.

Size

4.8×1044

legal positions (Tromp). Tablebases solve every position with up to 7 pieces, by computation.

Not known

Which outcome holds from the initial position. “Chess is a draw” is a belief, not a result: recorded as correction C-1.

So G-3 splits: (a) prove determinacy (achieved) and (b) the value itself, an open problem that this project does not claim to solve.

Part 1

The rules as computable definitions

The rules of chess define a game, so they are definitions, not laws. The risk is fidelity, and fidelity is tested.

Kernel

Positions and legal moves

Squares are Fin 64; the board is a function from squares to pieces. Castling is the king’s two-square move; en passant is a pawn moving onto the en passant target. Everything is evaluated by the Lean kernel itself.

Sanity

Special moves

Castling: legal, not through an attacked square, not after the king has moved and returned.

En passant: only immediately after the double step; the captured pawn disappears.

Double check: only king moves are legal (exactly Kd8, Kf8, Kf7).

Also promotion (exactly four choices), pins, kings not adjacent, no capture of a defended piece. 40 fixtures in all.

Part 2

Perfect play

Chess is a finite game, so it has a value. What the value is remains unknown.

Theory

Zermelo, in general and for chess

The abstract part: backward induction on a game with a depth bound, one optimal positional strategy for both players. States carry the history, so this covers every strategy.

Theory

Why chess is finite: fivefold repetition

Positions are compared by a key: side to move, board, castling rights, set of legal moves. Keys form a finite type, and in a game that has not ended no key occurs five times, so the history is bounded.

The 75-move rule is not needed. The bound is astronomically large, and it does not matter: only finiteness is used.

Scale

4,096 kernel checks

  • One declaration per (white king, queen) square pair; 64 files, 64 rows each.
  • About 8 minutes per file, five in parallel: roughly 8.5 hours of kernel time.
What went wrong. A first version checked 4,096 entries per declaration: one Lean process grew to 11 GB and RAM filled up. The kernel’s caches are per declaration, so splitting the check fixed it. Each build ran under a memory cap; ulimit -v turned out to break Lean, a cgroup cap did not.

The full game

No repetition, no 75-move draw

A rank (twice the table depth with White to move, twice the largest reply depth plus one with Black to move) strictly decreases at every ply. Same position means same squares, hence same rank: nothing repeats. The rank is at most 20, so a clock of at most 130 never reaches 150.

Part 4

Dead positions

FIDE 5.2.2: no sequence of legal moves leads to checkmate. A semantic definition, proved by invariants.

Theory

Insufficient material

An invariant closed under legal moves that excludes checkmate. A king checked by a lone knight or bishop always has a flight square of the other colour, and the enemy king cannot cover it. Guard in the other direction: initial_not_dead.

Black is in check from the bishop, yet the position is dead.
